At Emory, I expanded that computer-vision work into 12-camera 3D motion capture, unsupervised behavioral modeling, and automated bioacoustics.

Computer vision · Unsupervised learning · Emory University · 2023–present

The grammar of movement

Can deep learning catch subtle motor problems in infant monkeys before a human observer can see them?

Juvenile macaques infected with Zika virus as infants lose their balance more often than control animals, but obvious slips only appear late. To detect subtler motor changes earlier, I designed a 12-camera capture setup and trained a 3D convolutional pose network (JARVIS-HybridNet in PyTorch) to track 17 keypoints across roughly one million frames.

Zika-infected macaques moved with less predictable sequences than controls, even though each individual movement lasted just as long. To uncover this pattern without human checklists, I retuned an unsupervised sequence model (Keypoint-MoSeq) from mice to macaques, segmenting 3D pose streams into 15 recurring movement “syllables” and modeling transitions between them.

1. Capture. Twelve synchronized cameras surround a play cage and record freely moving macaques at up to 60 frames per second, keeping occluded limbs visible from multiple angles.

2. Label and detect. I wrote the annotation protocol and selected over 65,000 diverse frames for hand labeling; a neural network then detects 17 keypoints in every camera view.

3. Reconstruct in 3D. I trained a 3D convolutional network (JARVIS-HybridNet, PyTorch) on multi-GPU clusters to fuse all views into one 3D skeleton, averaging 13.75 mm of error (less than the width of a dime) across about one million frames.

4. Discover behavioral structure. I retuned Keypoint-MoSeq to macaque movement timescales, discovering 15 recurring “syllables” whose transition patterns separate Zika-infected animals from controls.

17 keypoints · 13.75 mm average 3D error · 65,000+ hand-labeled frames · ~1 million frames analyzed

Selected for a Society for Neuroscience 2026 press conference, one of 30–40 abstracts chosen from 8,000–10,000 submitted. Supported by a $50K pilot grant (Co-PI).

Bayesian statistics · Emory University · 2023–present

Zika and the developing brain

What does a Zika virus infection in the first weeks of life do to a developing brain?

I led the statistical analysis for an eight-month longitudinal study of 24 infant rhesus macaques—12 infected with Zika virus at five weeks old and 12 controls—measured across brain MRI, immune markers, vision, stress, and attachment. Because small cohorts demand rigorous uncertainty quantification, I built Bayesian hierarchical models (R, brms, Stan) with priors informed by earlier healthy-infant studies and reported full posterior distributions rather than binary significance tests.

Zika-infected infants matched controls on visual acuity at four and six months, but they showed poorer early emotional regulation, unusual hostility during mild stress, blunted separation distress, and wider cerebrospinal fluid spaces around the brain on MRI at three months. Every analysis script, prior specification, sensitivity check, and dataset is public.

  • R / brms / Stan

Bioacoustics · Bayesian statistics · Emory University · 2024–present

What a scream carries

Infant macaques scream when separated from their caregivers. Do Zika-infected infants sound different when they do?

Zika-infected infants matched controls in scream pitch and vocal steadiness, yet two acoustic differences survived stress testing. Zika-infected infants concentrated scream energy at lower frequencies and in a narrower band, and Zika-infected males gave fewer back-to-back calls than control males (11–36% vs. 38–81%). To separate real signal from noise, I fitted 36 Bayesian hierarchical models in PyMC across 5,328 calls from 24 infants and ran five rounds of sensitivity checks.

Because four annotators had to hand-mark 10,118 calls, I also built an automated PyTorch audio detector (CNN + bidirectional GRU on log-mel spectrograms) to scale future studies. Evaluated via cross-validation on held-out animals it has never heard, the detector identifies call boundaries and call types directly from raw audio.

Open dataset · University of Minnesota · 2022–2023

OpenApePose

Pose-tracking models learned what a body looks like from photographs of people. What happens when the body belongs to an orangutan?

I created OpenApePose (eLife, 2023), an open dataset of 71,868 photographs across six ape species labeled with 16 body landmarks. Leading the project end to end, I coordinated roughly 10 remote photographers across zoos and sanctuaries, managed commercial annotation quality control, and curated images for pose diversity rather than raw count.

I trained and benchmarked six deep pose-estimation architectures in MMPose on OpenApePose, and my best model (HRNet-W48) substantially outperformed models trained on close relatives. On unseen ape photographs, this OpenApePose model reached an accuracy score (AUC) of 0.90 out of 1.0, compared with 0.74 for a monkey-trained model and 0.71 for a human-trained model. When I held out an entire ape species from training, the OpenApePose model still beat the monkey-trained baseline on every unseen species.

Field research · Causal inference · Ph.D., 2015–2022

The dialects that were not there

Chimpanzees can pick out a stranger’s call from hundreds of meters away. Are they hearing a foreign dialect, or just a voice they do not recognize?

Contrary to earlier reports of chimpanzee dialects, I found no reliable vocal differences between neighboring communities at Gombe National Park, Tanzania. Working with two field assistants I trained, we recorded 1,252 pant-hoot calls from two neighboring communities and compared them with recordings from Uganda (325 screened calls across 18 males). I used causal diagrams (DAGs) to select control variables, ran a 1,000-shuffle permutation test so talkative males could not inflate group accuracy, and audited the sample for collection bias.

Instead of group dialects, pant-hoots strongly encoded individual identity: classifiers identified individual males at roughly twice the shuffled baseline. That result offers a simpler explanation for playback experiments, because a chimpanzee only needs to remember the voices of its own group to spot a stranger. I published the replication with all code and acoustic measurements open.

Bioacoustics · Machine learning · Ph.D., 2017–2022

What counts as a bark?

Researchers sort chimpanzee calls into types by ear. Would a computer, given only the sound, draw the same lines?

For the first chapter of my Ph.D. dissertation, I tested whether machine-learning features borrowed from human speech recognition (MFCCs) classify chimpanzee calls better than traditional pitch measurements. Across 281 archival Gombe calls spanning five types, I compared discriminant analysis, random forests, and a small neural network, and then clustered the calls without any labels.

Neither feature set nor classifier clearly won: every pairing matched human labels on roughly three calls in four (71–78% test accuracy). Pant-hoots and screams separated cleanly, while barks blurred into waa-barks and screams. Left without labels, unsupervised clustering grouped the five human-named call types into three natural clusters rather than five.

Data science across fields · 2015–present

Statistical collaborations

How do you turn messy experimental and field data across disciplines into conclusions the data can actually support?

I have served as analysis lead or statistical advisor on 19 research projects across neuroscience, archaeology, psychology, and ecology. Specific projects include modeling how stone-tool edge curvature affects slicing efficiency, validating how larger territories lower mortality risk in wolves and chimpanzees, and comparing male aggression between wild bonobos and chimpanzees.

In each collaboration, I start by drawing a causal diagram to identify confounders before fitting mixed-effects or Bayesian models and documenting what the data cannot prove. These collaborations have produced peer-reviewed papers in Current Biology, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, and Ecological Modelling, most with reproducible public code.
