Computer vision · Emory University · 2023–present

The grammar of movement

Some movement problems are easy to see. The ones I am after hide in plain sight.

I designed and built a 12-camera system and deep-learning pipeline that reconstructs freely moving infant rhesus macaques in 3D, to detect motor problems too subtle to see by eye. A second model treats movement like language: given no labels, it breaks movement into short “syllables,” and I study the “grammar” those syllables follow.

1. Capture. 12 machine-vision cameras, hardware-synchronized at up to 60 frames per second, streaming uncompressed video over a 120 Gbps network.

2. Label and detect. 65,000+ frames hand-labeled under a protocol I wrote, then a network finds 17 keypoints in every camera view.

3. Reconstruct in 3D. The views are combined into one 3D pose by triangulation (built on JARVIS, PyTorch). Average error is 13.75 mm, and the model has been run on about a million frames.

4. Find structure. An unsupervised model (Keypoint-MoSeq tuned for macaques) splits movement into short “syllables” that make up behaviors such as walking and climbing.

17 keypoints · 13.75 mm average 3D error · 65,000+ hand-labeled frames · about 1 million frames analyzed

Selected for a Society for Neuroscience 2026 press conference, one of 30–40 abstracts chosen from 8,000–10,000 submitted. Supported by a $50K pilot grant (Co-PI).

Bayesian statistics · Emory University · 2023–present

Zika and the developing brain

What does an infection in the first weeks of life do to a brain that is still being built?

A study of postnatal Zika virus infection, using infant rhesus macaque models. The same animals were measured in many ways (brain MRI, immune markers, viral load, behavior), and I analyzed each with Bayesian hierarchical models (brms) that adjust for confounders, with priors built from earlier studies.

Every result is reported as a distribution with its uncertainty, and all of the code and data are public.

  • Bayesian hierarchical models
  • R / brms / Stan
  • Small-sample longitudinal data
  • Reproducible analysis

Bioacoustics · Bayesian statistics · Emory University · 2024–present

What a scream carries

Infant macaques scream when they are kept apart from the person who raised them. Do infants infected with Zika virus sound different when they do?

I measured 15 acoustic features in 5,328 calls from 24 infants and fitted a Bayesian model to each. Then I tried to break the results. What survived was a change in the shape of the scream's spectrum and in the rhythm of calling, not in its pitch.

Alongside the study I built a PyTorch detector that finds and labels calls automatically, tested on animals it has never heard.

Open dataset · University of Minnesota · 2022–2023

OpenApePose

Pose-tracking models learned from pictures of people. What happens when the body belongs to an orangutan?

An open dataset of 71,868 photographs of six ape species, each labeled with 16 body landmarks, published in eLife. I led it end to end: collecting images at zoos and sanctuaries with about 10 remote photographers, managing annotation with a commercial service, and choosing images for variety of poses instead of raw count.

A model trained on it scored 0.90 on unseen ape photographs, against 0.74 for a monkey-trained model and 0.71 for a human-trained one, and it still beat the monkey-trained model on a species held out of training.

  • HRNet / ResNet

Field research · Causal inference · Ph.D., 2015–2022

The dialects that were not there

Chimpanzees can pick out a stranger’s call from hundreds of meters away. Are they hearing a foreign dialect, or just a voice they do not know?

With a field team I trained at Gombe National Park, Tanzania, we recorded 1,252 pant-hoot calls from two neighboring chimpanzee communities, which I compared with recordings from Uganda. Using causal diagrams (DAGs), permutation tests and an audit of sampling bias, I found no reliable community differences.

What the calls carried instead was the caller: strong individual signatures. I published the contrary result, and all of my dissertation data are public.

  • Acoustic feature extraction

Bioacoustics · Machine learning · Ph.D., 2017–2022

What counts as a bark?

Researchers name chimpanzee calls by ear. Would a computer, given only the sound, draw the same lines?

For the first chapter of my dissertation I took 281 archival calls of five types and measured each one two ways: with the field’s traditional pitch measurements, and with the features used in human speech recognition (MFCCs). I compared discriminant analysis, random forests and a small neural network, then clustered the calls with no labels at all.

Nothing clearly won. About three calls in four were named correctly whatever I used. Pant-hoots and screams were easy and barks were not, and left to itself the computer found three groups, not five.

  • Audio features (MFCCs)
  • Random forests
  • Neural networks
  • Clustering

About

I am a data scientist who trained as a biologist. I build computer-vision and audio models that measure what animals do, and I use Bayesian statistics to work out what those measurements mean. I am a Postdoctoral Fellow at Emory University in Atlanta and a part-time master's student in computer science at Georgia Tech.

I have been interested in wildlife since I was a child, and I studied biology and mathematics at IISER Pune in India. My first research jobs there were surveying birds up a mountainside in Arunachal Pradesh and writing simulations of when plants flower.

For my Ph.D. at the University of Minnesota, I recorded wild chimpanzees in Tanzania with a field team I trained. Collecting the recordings turned out to be the easier half. To analyze them I taught myself statistics and machine learning from textbooks and online courses, and along the way I became the person other researchers came to with their data: 19 projects so far, in fields from archaeology to neuroscience.

A postdoc in a neuroscience lab at Minnesota moved me into deep learning, where I built OpenApePose. At Emory I designed a 3D motion-capture system from the cameras up, and I lead the statistics for a study of how early Zika virus infection affects the developing brain.
